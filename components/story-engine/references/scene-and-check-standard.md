# Scene and Check Standard

This standard governs scene-level craft: box text, world concreteness, directionality, NPC knowledge, dungeon rooms, check adjudication, consequences, and pacing. It does not set DC numbers, encounter math, rewards, or clock mechanics (`mechanical-floor.md`), adventure-level structure (`structure-patterns.md`), manuscript order or layout (`module-format-standard.md`), or setting posture (`realms-grounding.md`). Quality is judged by `ben-grade-gate.md`.

## Box Text

Pithy box text is not merely short — pithy means economical, not just brief. It places characters in action and implies playable options through immediate NPCs, dangers, pressures, objects, exits, and affordances. It gives the DM a natural checkpoint to ask "What do you do?"

Rules:

1. **Pithy, not merely short.** Information economy is the measure, not length.
2. **Describe character experience.** What characters see, hear, smell, feel, or can immediately infer from evidence.
3. **Make it actionable.** Include visible objects, threats, NPC movement, exits, or interactable features when possible.
4. **Do not explain hidden lore.** Hidden causes and mechanics go in the DM-facing procedure after the text.
5. **Do not prescribe player emotion.** No "you feel afraid." Describe the evidence instead.
6. **Do not assume player actions.** Avoid "as you enter" when the party may scout or approach another way. Use flexible framing: "From the threshold or nearest safe vantage."
7. **Do not resolve the scene.** The text frames; play decides.
8. **Prefer poetic compression over purple abstraction.** A concrete image beats an abstract thesis. Good: "The bucket rises wet from a dry well." Bad: "The ancient contradiction of water and ownership manifests symbolically."
9. **Every major scene and keyed area gets box text.** Minor optional features may use one sentence.
10. **Follow text with procedure.** After box text: what is happening, the important interactables, consequential checks, clues, complications, and exits in the order play is likely to expose them. Do not enumerate ordinary player actions.
11. **Box text lives in the scene.** Front matter orients the DM; Scene 1's opening text, checks, and consequences belong in Scene 1's entry.
12. **One label per packet.** Use `Pithy Box Text` or `Read or Paraphrase`, consistently throughout.

Box text check: Can the DM read it without decoding lore? Does it invite an action? Does it avoid assuming how the party entered? Does it leave the outcome unresolved? Is the image concrete enough to remember?

## World Concreteness and Naming

Every important person, place, thing, and concept must be playable, historical, emotional, and spatially concrete — at a scale matching its importance. A mythic object, named ritual, strange monster, or central handout needs deeper completion. Ordinary facts may do ordinary work: a furnishing can establish use, a creature's behavior can explain ecology, and a simple observed fact can orient the players in a place.

Do not turn every noun, furnishing, or room detail into a bespoke mechanic, check, complication, or hidden interaction. Do not frame dressing as a passage, special affordance, or secret unless the procedure supports it; keep undiscovered secrets in DM-facing procedure. Apply the deeper completion test to elements that carry the scene's pressure, choice, clue, route, reward, or special rule.

- **Playable:** it creates or clarifies something players can do. What action does it invite? What roll, spell, tool, risk, or bargain interacts with it? Does it create choice, consequence, clue, pressure, route, or reward?
- **Historical:** it existed before the characters arrived. Who made, named, used, broke, wants, or misunderstands it? What would a local, a scholar, and an enemy each call it?
- **Emotional:** it matters to someone. Who fears, needs, would lie for, or would kill for it? What does it cost, threaten, or promise?
- **Spatial:** it has presence. Where does it sit on the map? What blocks sight or movement to it? Does it need a handout, token, or map feature?

**Revision rule.** For each important element, decide: **Keep** (concrete enough for its role), **Complete** (important role, needs more grounding), **Demote** (name or concept inflated beyond its table function), or **Remove** (flavor without action, clue, pressure, route, reward, or emotional weight).

**Naming.** A fantasy name is not a finished thought; it is a design challenge. Either complete the myth until the name is earned, or demote the term to plain or local language — never swap one vague fantasy compound for another. If everything is mythically named, nothing is mythic.

- **Plain names remain powerful:** the coffer, the brass token, the old stair, the road bell. Use plain language when a thing's table function is practical and clear.
- **Local names often beat grand names:** owl-door if goblins say it, the bad stair if locals fear it. Local names reveal who uses them and why.
- **Historical names need history:** use "the Netherese stair" only when the adventure establishes who built, used, or misunderstood the thing.
- **Mythic names are rare and costly:** use mythic register only when the adventure supports it through religion, prophecy, old-empire history, repeated local belief, a handout or inscription, or a mechanical scene function players experience.

Completion test for any grand term: What hazard, choice, clue, route, bargain, or reward does it create? What adjudication handle does it have? Does it matter on a map or handout? Does it pay off later? If not, demote it.

**Plain, table-clear language beats forced flavor coinages.** When a plain phrase and an invented coinage compete, use the plain one (recorded ruling: "false road signal" kept; the coinage "false patrol call" rejected).

## The Scene Engine

A scene is finished when it gives players meaningful things to do, not when it explains what happens. If a scene reads well but would leave players mostly listening, revise it.

**Scene purpose.** Name what the scene is for at the table: start with danger, deliver a clue through action, create a social choice, choose a route, spend resources, force a consequence, set up the climax. If the purpose is only "explain backstory," convert the backstory into a clue, NPC move, object, hazard, or choice.

**Player-facing action.** Make immediate affordances concrete: the burning map can be grabbed, the suspicious floor tested, the ritual interrupted, the guard bargained with, the treasure taken at the risk of an alarm. A generic "What Characters Can Do" list is a smell. State the situation and any special procedure clearly enough that ordinary choices follow from the fiction.

**Invitation.** A concrete opening gives players a recognizable reason to lean in — curiosity, danger, rescue, gain, rivalry, or opportunity can each do it. These are alternatives, not a quota or a demand for a combat, hunt, tragedy, patron, moral dilemma, or hidden deadline. An atmospheric or cooperative situation can be inviting when the reason to engage is legible.

**Touchstones, not checkboxes.** Scene depth comes from the right tools for that scene, not mandatory complexity. Use 2–4 fitting touchstones per major scene; never force all into any scene:

- **Object interaction:** a thing to touch, move, open, steal, break, or trigger.
- **NPC pressure/conversation:** someone with wants, leverage, a secret, a price, or a deadline.
- **Tactical movement/terrain:** cover, elevation, chokepoints, retreat routes.
- **Clue discovery:** information from searching, questioning, experimenting, tracking.
- **Clue jeopardy:** a clue at risk of being lost, distorted, stolen, or misunderstood if the party delays or mishandles it.
- **Resource expenditure:** time, hit points, spell slots, money, reputation.
- **Timing pressure:** visible countdown, moving enemy, spreading fire, patrol.
- **Moral choice:** a decision with social, personal, or practical cost.
- **Combat objective:** protect, steal, escape, interrupt, or hold — not only kill.
- **Map route choice:** alternate entrances, loops, shortcuts, bottlenecks.
- **Trap/puzzle manipulation:** visible handles to test, disable, or exploit.
- **Faction/social consequence:** who gains, who loses, who hears about it.
- **Environmental hazard:** fire, water, height, noise, darkness.
- **Treasure/risk tradeoff:** a visible reward that changes the room, route, or timer when taken.
- **Vivid table image, payoff echo, handout, or strange-but-understandable magic** when they serve the scene's purpose.

**Scene movement.** Each major scene needs at least one clear state change because players acted: the map is saved or burned; an NPC becomes ally, enemy, or liability; a route opens or closes; the clock advances; treasure is gained at a cost. If nothing changes after the players act, the scene is exposition.

**Scene exit.** State where the scene points next: a map location, an NPC lead, a handout clue, a visible landmark, a consequence that follows the party. The next lead must be obvious enough that the DM never improvises the spine.

**Complexity rule.** Complexity is acceptable when it increases playable choices — more ways to test, bypass, or exploit; consequences from visible decisions; different character types contributing. First reduce the facts and dependencies a cold DM must hold together at once, not merely the word count. A few understandable fictional facts should explain rich interactions; more options do not excuse an opaque underlying situation. Complexity is bad when the DM must explain abstract lore before players can act, terms lack handles, or logic is hidden from observation.

**Fix patterns.** Flat scene: add a thing to do now, a vivid physical change, or a consequence for waiting. Confusing scene: remove one invented term, collapse two factions into one pressure, make the next destination visible — remove concepts before adding explanation. Overbuilt scene: keep the two most useful touchstones, move optional lore to a later clue. If a scene requires a glossary to run, simplify it.

## The Scene Check

Every major scene must be able to answer, for a cold DM:

1. What box text opens this scene, and does it describe what characters perceive without deciding what they do?
2. What starts the scene?
3. What is visibly at stake?
4. What can players do immediately?
5. What changes if they do nothing?
6. What route or decision carries play forward, and are alternatives visible when the location actually has them?
7. Do the checks state what success and failure change?
8. Are the important named elements concrete enough to use at the table?

If a scene cannot answer these, revise it, combine it with another scene, or cut it.

## Directionality

Direction means the adventure always has visible traction; railroading means only one player choice works. A one-shot needs clear direction and meaningful agency, not railroading.

- **Clear opening pressure.** The adventure must be explainable in one sentence, and the first decision point must arrive inside the first 10 minutes of play.
- **One practical lore-delivery vehicle** tells the party what is happening, why it matters, where to go, and what to do first. It may be an NPC, object, map, corpse, handout, faction order, captured prisoner, or battlefield aftermath. Check it: What does it tell the party in the first 10 minutes? Why should they care? Where does it point? How does it pay off later? If the answer is "it explains theme," revise it into something practical.
- **Support the situation, not three scripted routes.** Diplomacy, investigation, force, retreat, or another approach should work when the fiction supports it, but the manuscript does not owe every scene a route matrix. State the people, pressures, objects, and custom rules; write an explicit branch only when its result is special or load-bearing.
- **Avoid fixed outcomes.** Write consequences instead of dead ends, multiple clue vectors, NPCs who react to choices, and a climax objective solvable through combat, bargain, trickery, sacrifice, or exposure where appropriate. The climax must pay off earlier clues, choices, or risks.
- **At most one visible tracker.** If a custom mechanic or tracker exists, players must encounter it by the halfway mark or it gets cut. Clock mechanics live in `mechanical-floor.md`.
- **Maps change decisions.** A table map shows the start, visible routes and exits, the objective, hazards, and interactables. If a map does not change decisions, it is a mood image. Manuscript presentation belongs to `module-format-standard.md`.
- **Traction sources:** a person asks for something now; a place changes while players watch; a timer advances; the next clue is physically reachable; the antagonist's next move is understandable.
- **Table spark.** Each one-shot needs one interactive moment the table will retell: a cup of water turns to glass in a player's hand, a monster offers a bargain mid-combat.

Prefer simple plots with strong execution over complex structures. If players cannot identify a clear next action, the scene is not directional enough.

## Re-entry After Changed Play

A strong situation remains useful after the party changes the expected course. After improvisation, a bargain, a retreat, or a changed location, regain bearings from the fiction now true: who is present and what they want, what visibly threatens or offers, and which object, route, relationship, or consequence still matters. Present that situation and let the players decide what to investigate, discuss, or do. This can give the DM breathing room to think or consult the module during productive improvisation; it need not rescue stalled players or interrupt what they are already pursuing.

Do not rescue the plan by inventing a fallback scene, reviving a missed beat, explaining away a mystery, or steering the party back to a predetermined route. Re-entry succeeds when an existing, evocative situation is intelligible enough to invite action while preserving the changed state. It is a way for the DM to find their footing, not extra content or a list of actions the party must take.

## Consequence Doctrine

Consequences replace dead ends, but not every category belongs in every adventure. During design, test delay, refusal, negotiation, force, stealth failure, and social failure. In the manuscript, state only the consequences that are likely, custom, or load-bearing; let ordinary system consequences remain ordinary.

- **Delay:** a clock advances, an NPC makes a claim, a path becomes dangerous, a resource spoils.
- **Refusal:** another faction acts, the hook returns sharper, an innocent pays a cost, the characters lose leverage.
- **Negotiation:** the party gains a route but owes something, reveals a name, accepts a restriction, or shifts faction balance.
- **Force/violence:** witnesses react, law or treaty terms trigger, collateral damage changes the ending, a combat objective becomes harder.
- **Stealth failure:** must say who notices, what they do, and whether combat actually starts. State what the noticer does first — shout, run, bargain, attack, lock a door, move a hostage, advance a timer, call reinforcements — and what choices the players still have. Never imply an unspecified fight: if failure starts combat, define the encounter, enemy objective, starting positions, and a noncombat escape or recovery path.
- **Social failure:** must give the DM a usable table move — what the NPC gives anyway, refuses, demands, distorts, or does next — never a blank wall.

## Check Adjudication

**Naked DCs are not acceptable. Every suggested check needs adjudication: success, failure, cost, partial result, and how the scene continues.** Never write `DC 13 Investigation: read the tally` alone.

Required shape for every meaningful check:

- **Trigger:** when the check is called for.
- **DC and ability:** or a small menu of plausible abilities/tools. Numeric calibration comes from the DC bands in `mechanical-floor.md`.
- **Success:** what the character gains, prevents, opens, or changes.
- **Failure:** what complication, cost, delay, damage, alert, or partial information follows.
- **Fail-forward path:** how the scene continues without blocking the adventure.

In the DM-facing manuscript, prefer prose over tables for ordinary checks, placed where the action happens: "A character who studies the mud can make a DC 13 Survival or Investigation check. On a success, they identify the Old Coach Road and the rope-burned knot. On a failure, they still identify the road, but miss the duress clue until Tamsin sees the knot." Use a compact table only for a true special procedure, a timer, or many simultaneous options.

**Essential clues.** Never gate essential progress behind one failed roll. Every essential clue needs at least two delivery paths among: NPC knowledge, physical evidence, handout, map feature, monster behavior, room clue, or consequence. On a failed roll: the party gets the clue with a cost, gets a partial clue plus another path, or another vehicle delivers the same direction while pressure worsens. Failure should rarely mean "nothing happens."

**Stealth checks:** apply the stealth-failure entry of the Consequence Doctrine in full.

**Social checks** should not replace conversation. State what the NPC already wants, what leverage changes the DC, what they give on success, what they give/refuse/demand/distort/do on failure, and whether intimidation, bribery, honesty, proof, rank, or shared values changes the outcome.

**Hazard and object checks** must include: what players can see before rolling, how to test it safely, what tools, spells, or clever actions bypass the roll, the cost on failure, and whether retrying is possible and at what cost.

**Smell tests — revise if:** a check has no consequence; failure blocks the next scene; stealth failure implies a vague fight; social failure gives no table move; a scene has many DCs but no state changes; the packet says "players can investigate" without saying what they find.

## NPC Knowledge Blocks

Place a plot-facing NPC's useful knowledge beside the first likely conversation so the DM can answer without searching. Use a separate knowledge block only when the NPC recurs across separated scenes and the block is genuinely faster than adjacent prose. Minor monsters and pure combatants need only tactics, morale, and treasure.

When a recurring NPC earns a `What <NPC> Knows` block, include only the facts and reactions that matter tonight; do not force a seven-prompt questionnaire.

A knowledge block should answer: What is happening? Why should the party care? Where can the party go? What can they do first? What changes if they ignore this? Blocks support play; they are not lore essays.

**Voice separation.** Keep NPC voice separate from information: a **Voice/handle** line and a **One line to say**. Never bury essential facts only inside stylized dialogue.

If a missed NPC conversation would break the adventure spine, add another delivery path (see Essential Clues).

## Dungeon Rooms

A keyed room must be a playable situation, not a description. Every keyed room gives players something to DO, and opens with box text describing the first usable experience from a flexible vantage.

**Correspondence sequence.** Build the room from **box text → dungeon features → map features**. Let the opening make its usable, perceptible nouns clear. Reuse a recognizable opening noun for a feature heading only when that feature has local behavior, an interaction, a consequence, or a route to carry; keep undiscovered secrets out of the read-aloud. The map must visibly support that same feature. Neither a heading for absent art nor art without a runnable feature satisfies the sequence.

**Dominant assets need a room.** A large altar, machine, tree, ship, or other focal asset needs authored supporting space and relationships—how it is approached, worked, blocked, overlooked, escaped, or contested—rather than an empty container or pasted illustration. Choose only the features the room needs; do not add a decoration quota or force every room into the same list.

**Room touchstones** (choose the few that make the room playable, never all): **Door** (how players enter, detect, force, or avoid the room), **Danger** (what can hurt, alarm, delay, trap, or mislead), **Toy** (what players can touch, test, move, open, steal, or break), **Clue** (what the room teaches about the dungeon, threat, or later payoff), **Choice** (what decision matters here), **Cost** (what time, resource, risk, or noise the room demands — resolve via the Consequence Doctrine), **Reward** (treasure, route access, ally, leverage, or funny victory), **Exit** (where the room points next — if players leave asking "now what?", the room needs a clearer lead).

**Empty rooms** may exist only to create pacing, tension, route choice, foreshadowing, safety after danger, or a place where noise or pursuit matters. A room that only says "nothing is here" gets cut or merged.

**Traps** are telegraphed enough to be played, not merely suffered: a tell, a way to test, avoid, disable, or exploit, and a cost for rushing. No default gotcha traps without an observable handle. **Tricks need handles:** a visible oddity, repeated symbol, environmental tell, safe testing method, or prior clue.

**Puzzles** produce table talk: visible goal, manipulable pieces, partial progress, multiple solution routes, and fail-forward with cost instead of a dead end.

**Monsters** need a role beyond hit points where possible: a room job, a route they protect, a fear, a bargain, a reason to flee, talk, or escalate. If a creature delivers plot information, give it a knowledge block; if it only fights, tactics and morale suffice. Stat blocks and encounter math live in `mechanical-floor.md`.

**Site identity and variety.** Players should understand what kind of place they entered within the first few minutes — trick maze, cursed shrine, goblin-rigged ruin — through the entrance, first room, motifs, treasure logic, or dungeon behavior. The place should feel authored by someone or something. Vary room modes (combat, trap, puzzle, social, weird object, temptation, false safety, clue); the mix matters more than the count. The dungeon must be fun without deep lore: testing rooms, choosing routes, handling tricks, taking risks, claiming treasure.

**Map flow.** Branching, loops, bottlenecks, and shortcuts let players choose routes, create "do we risk that door?" moments, allow retreat, and let consequences travel through the dungeon. Place memorable rooms near decisions.

**Payoffs.** Earlier rooms should help later rooms: a clue explains a later hazard, an object solves another room, a spared creature returns. **Treasure is a gameplay tool** — it tempts, teaches, unlocks, misleads, or changes risk, not just payout; reward values come from `mechanical-floor.md`.

**Return state.** For important rooms, know what changes if players return: trap spent, creature fled or reinforced, door locked, alarm active.

## Pacing

A one-shot should feel complete even if the table runs slow. Do not spend the first hour explaining lore, theme, or special terms.

- **2 hours:** hook 10 min; scene 1 with immediate choice and first clue 25; social/puzzle/exploration pressure 35; climax 35; fallout 15. One strong location, one major turn, one concrete job, one primary complication.
- **3 hours:** hook 10; arrival/exploration 35; social or puzzle pressure 45; climax 55; fallout 25. One medium dungeon, faction knot, or investigation with a decisive final scene.
- **4 hours:** hook 10; opening scene 40; exploration/investigation 50; social or faction pressure 50; climax 70; epilogue 20. Full module-style one-shot with several approaches.

These beat tables total the full slot; the playable-content budget from references/mechanical-floor.md (slot minus 30 minutes) should be protected by knowing what content can be omitted or deepened if play changes. Those options are tools, not automatic events.

**Pacing nutrition.** Do not force equal pillar time; balance the meal. Mostly social: embody danger in clocks, leverage, or a crisis. Mostly combat: make stakes social or moral. Mostly exploration: include a speaking pressure and a risk that moves without the party. Mostly mystery: make clues physical and consequences immediate. Simple does not mean flat — even a light one-shot needs one vivid place, one memorable image, one immediately understandable challenge, and one clear payoff.

**Pacing reads.** Do not move a clue, make an NPC reveal a route, or trigger a climax solely because a fraction of the slot has elapsed. First distinguish productive sandbox expansion from a stall. When players are meaningfully investigating, discussing, or acting and their choices are changing the fiction, let that play breathe. When the table is genuinely confused or stalled, regain traction from observable facts, an established pressure, or a lead already supported by the situation; do not manufacture rescue content or restart a missed scene.

When a one-shot has a promised ending and time is genuinely short, use an established fictional deadline, consequence, or currently true situation to bring the existing pressure toward a decision. Do not force a missed checkpoint or erase the party's changed state. Expansion material and cut-first material remain available to the DM according to the table's choices, not as automatic switches.

## Pillar Profiles — Design Notes Only

Pillar tags and design-category labels live in design notes and QA files only — never in the DM-facing manuscript, per scene or per room. Scenes show their balance through playable text; the manuscript reads like an adventure, not a design document.

In design notes, declare a pillar profile (primary, secondary, tertiary/light among combat, exploration, roleplay) and optionally a scene pillar map to check balance. Weighting follows the premise, and lesser does not mean absent. If exploration is primary, scenes need movement, map choices, and environmental handling; if combat is primary, objectives, terrain, and failure that continues play; if roleplay is primary, NPC agendas, social pressure, and multiple ways to learn essential facts. Quality review judges whether the weighting fits the premise, not whether pillars are equal.
