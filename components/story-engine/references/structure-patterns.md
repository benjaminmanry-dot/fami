# Structure Patterns

This file owns two things: the library of 12 reusable bounded-adventure structures, and the single canonical structure-rating rubric. No other file defines a structure rubric; sibling files reference this one by filename.

These are reusable structures, not formulas to obey blindly. Choose the pattern that makes the first 10 minutes clearest, lowers prep burden, and creates obvious player action.

## Selection Principles

- Do not default to a complex structure when a simple one works.
- Pick the structure that makes the first 10 minutes clearest, gives the least prep burden, makes the maps and handouts useful, creates clear player action, and supports the concept's strongest table image.
- Structure serves table play, not abstract cleverness.

## Opening Rules

- If the adventure opens with combat or crisis, the clue or lore-delivery vehicle must point clearly to the next location.
- If the adventure opens with an NPC job offer, the NPC must be practical, memorable, and able to keep play moving. Plain "you are hired" openings are banned unless the job itself contains pressure, leverage, or a choice.
- If the adventure opens with a handout, map, object, corpse, public disaster, or visible event, it must answer where to go and why.
- If the first 10 minutes require backstory before player action, choose a different structure.

## Optional Five-Room Functional Lens

Use this lens only when a compact keyed site would benefit from it. It tests whether the site changes mode and pays itself off; it is not a demand for five literal rooms or a linear map.

1. **Entrance or guardian:** establishes the site's identity, objective, and first resistance.
2. **Puzzle or social test:** asks for observation, leverage, experimentation, or negotiation rather than another damage exchange.
3. **Setback, reversal, or costly choice:** changes what the party believes, owns, owes, or can safely do.
4. **Climax:** concentrates the site's active opposition around an objective more specific than reducing hit points.
5. **Revelation or reward:** pays off an earlier clue, choice, bargain, risk, or strange element and points to the next state.

Functions may share a room, occur in another order, sit on different branches, or be omitted when the site's own logic does the job better. Add a visible timer only when the fiction needs one; pursuit, rival movement, an environmental change, or a human cost can provide pressure without a track.

## The Pattern Library

Each entry states when to use it and everything the structure needs to run. The **Best when** line doubles as the selection heuristic.

### 1. Road Job / Caravan Route

- **Best when:** the adventure is about travel, escort, dangerous terrain, trade routes, or getting people or cargo somewhere — travel pressure is the fun.
- **Lore delivery:** practical guide, route map, caravan contract, road shrine, ambush survivor, damaged wagon.
- **Opening:** job offer or route crisis. If it opens as a job offer, the job-offer ban above applies: the job must arrive carrying pressure, leverage, or a choice — not a flat hiring scene.
- **Middle:** route complications, weather/terrain pressure, a social or toll choice, an attack or clue on the road.
- **Climax:** arrival under pressure, final roadblock, destination reveal, or enemy ambush.
- **Maps:** road encounter, camp/waystation, destination or final pass.
- **Handouts:** route map, contract, warning note, faction order.
- **Agency:** choose route, pace, who to trust, what risks to take, what to protect.
- **Payoff:** arrive safely, save cargo or people, earn a contact, reveal a future route threat.
- **Pitfall:** becomes a string of random encounters.
- **Keep it runnable:** every road scene must change resources, route, trust, or the final encounter.

### 2. Ambush With A Clue

- **Best when:** the concept has a strong combat/crisis opening and a physical clue that points forward, revealing the adventure through evidence.
- **Lore delivery:** map on an attacker, coded order, stolen object, wounded prisoner, strange monster mutation, battlefield aftermath.
- **Opening:** ambush, monster attack, raid, chase, or sudden rescue.
- **Middle:** identify the clue, follow the trail, question a survivor, choose a pursuit route.
- **Climax:** enemy hideout, ruin chamber, ritual site, or boss with evidence.
- **Maps:** ambush site, trail/approach, final site.
- **Handouts:** crude map, orders, blood-marked tally, stolen token.
- **Agency:** how to handle captives, where to pursue, whether to warn locals or rush.
- **Payoff:** the clue makes sense, the ambushers' motive is revealed, the immediate threat is stopped.
- **Pitfall:** the clue is missed, or the combat feels disconnected.
- **Keep it runnable:** make the clue impossible to miss and have it physically point to the next location.

### 3. Local Disaster

- **Best when:** the strongest image is a public problem the characters cannot ignore.
- **Lore delivery:** public disaster, visible magical event, collapsing building, poisoned well, plague mark, weather anomaly.
- **Opening:** public crisis and triage.
- **Middle:** local choices, a witness or object that explains, source investigation, a pressure clock.
- **Climax:** stop the source, rescue trapped people, expose the cause, repair a broken ward.
- **Maps:** public location, investigation route, source site.
- **Handouts:** damaged notice, victim list, ritual fragment, old plan.
- **Agency:** triage, rescue priority, investigation path, who receives help first.
- **Payoff:** the disaster ends or stabilizes; the community changes because of player choices.
- **Pitfall:** too many NPCs or civic details.
- **Keep it runnable:** keep the first problem visible and the source concrete.

### 4. Missing Person / Rescue Trail

- **Best when:** the emotional core is rescuing someone or finding out what happened to them.
- **Lore delivery:** the missing person's pack, last message, witness child, animal companion, abandoned cart, trail signs.
- **Opening:** request for help, discovered evidence, or an immediate rescue clue.
- **Middle:** evidence trail, false lead, dangerous location approach.
- **Climax:** rescue, reveal, hostage exchange, monster nest, or trapped chamber.
- **Maps:** starting location, trail/route, rescue site.
- **Handouts:** last letter, sketch, route note, clue object.
- **Agency:** follow tracks, question suspects, choose fast or safe route, negotiate or force the rescue.
- **Payoff:** person saved, truth revealed, a relationship or faction changes.
- **Pitfall:** becomes a single-track railroad.
- **Keep it runnable:** give multiple clue vectors that all point toward the rescue site.

### 5. Map To A Ruin

- **Best when:** the adventure should be exploration-forward with a clear destination, and a handout map can carry the whole spine.
- **Lore delivery:** recovered map, treasure rubbing, enemy sketch, old survey, bought relic, tattoo map.
- **Opening:** map discovery, contested auction, ambush over the map, or a patron hands it over.
- **Middle:** travel/approach, visible ruin features, keyed areas.
- **Climax:** hidden chamber, cache, awakened guardian, or a rival arrives.
- **Maps:** approach, ruin exterior, keyed interior.
- **Handouts:** the map itself, key inscription, survey notes.
- **Agency:** route choice, entry point, what to bypass, what to disturb.
- **Payoff:** the map proves true, a hidden chamber opens, a future site is named.
- **Pitfall:** ruin lore sprawls.
- **Keep it runnable:** the map is the spine; keep the objective visible.

### 6. Toll Gate / Blocked Passage

- **Best when:** route access — law, monsters, terrain, payment, or faction power — creates the main choice.
- **Lore delivery:** toll charter, bridge spirit, gate captain, road sign, blockade notice, trapped wagon.
- **Opening:** blocked route with immediate stakes.
- **Middle:** pay/fight/sneak/negotiate choice, its consequences, a hidden cause or ambush.
- **Climax:** the passage opens, the gate breaks, the authority is exposed, or a final attack at the crossing.
- **Maps:** gate/bridge/pass, nearby office or camp, undercroft or ambush site.
- **Handouts:** toll writ, pass token, counterfeit seal, warning notice.
- **Agency:** choose the method of passage and accept its consequences.
- **Payoff:** the road opens, the authority changes, a smuggling or faction hook is revealed.
- **Pitfall:** legal debate bogs down.
- **Keep it runnable:** keep a physical danger visible while the argument happens.

### 7. Faction Errand With Complication

- **Best when:** a clear organization job should become a field choice or loyalty problem.
- **Lore delivery:** faction order, patron, sealed message, courier, badge, captured operative.
- **Opening:** clear job from a faction, or discovery of a failed faction mission. The job-offer ban applies here too: a flat "the faction hires you" scene is banned unless the job itself contains pressure, leverage, or a choice.
- **Middle:** rival pressure, field complication, a moral or loyalty choice.
- **Climax:** choose allegiance, save the target, expose the patron, or finish the job on altered terms.
- **Maps:** briefing/contact site, field site, final complication site.
- **Handouts:** faction order, seal, target sketch, intercepted note.
- **Agency:** complete the job, revise it, betray the patron, negotiate rival terms.
- **Payoff:** faction reputation changes; job success has consequences.
- **Pitfall:** faction lore becomes homework.
- **Keep it runnable:** the job is one sentence; the complication is physical.

### 8. Siege / Holdout

- **Best when:** the fun is defending a location under pressure.
- **Lore delivery:** warning horn, wounded scout, visible enemy approach, refugee arrival, last messenger.
- **Opening:** arrival under pressure, or an alarm.
- **Middle:** prepare defenses, choose who or what to protect, wave or pressure events, social triage.
- **Climax:** final breach, leader duel, evacuation, sabotage, or counterstrike.
- **Maps:** defended site, breach points, final breach interior.
- **Handouts:** defense roster, wall map, enemy warning.
- **Agency:** allocate resources, build defenses, choose priorities, decide when to counterattack.
- **Payoff:** the site holds, partial loss, heroic evacuation, or a future alliance.
- **Pitfall:** too many waves or too much bookkeeping.
- **Keep it runnable:** use 3 defense choices and 3 pressure events, not a war game.

### 9. Festival / Public Event Turns Bad

- **Best when:** a social opening should become action in a crowded public space, and public reputation matters.
- **Lore delivery:** relic incident, public contest, cursed prize, witness, proclamation, visible omen.
- **Opening:** social event — contest, blessing, market, performance.
- **Middle:** public incident, suspects and pressure, a choice of whom to protect or accuse.
- **Climax:** public confrontation, ritual stop, chase, or exposing the culprit.
- **Maps:** square/event space, investigation route, final public site.
- **Handouts:** program, proclamation, relic inscription, suspect note.
- **Agency:** social choices, public reputation, who receives help, whom to accuse.
- **Payoff:** public trust changes; a festival tradition is altered or saved.
- **Pitfall:** the cast gets too large.
- **Keep it runnable:** center on one vivid incident and three major NPC pressures.

### 10. Compact Keyed Site

- **Best when:** a bounded location — dungeon, tower, shrine, fortress, lair, manor, or wreck — should carry the adventure through keyed play.
- **Lore delivery:** the entrance state, a trapped survivor, a rival's trail, site behavior, a damaged record, a resident creature, or an active ritual.
- **Opening:** a clear objective and first resistance at or immediately inside the site.
- **Middle:** route choices, distinct room modes, optional branches, shortcuts, and consequences that travel through the site.
- **Climax:** the site's active opposition concentrates around a concrete objective.
- **Maps:** a keyed site map with branches, reconnection points, bottlenecks, and at least one meaningful bypass when scale permits.
- **Handouts:** rough site map, keyed inscription, resident's warning, operating diagram, or claim record.
- **Agency:** entry method, route order, what to disturb or spare, which risk to accept, when to retreat or press.
- **Pressure:** optional. Use a visible timer only when its unit, trigger, thresholds, and endpoint arise naturally; otherwise use pursuit, rival movement, environmental change, or human cost.
- **Payoff:** an earlier room's clue, object, bargain, or risk changes the climax or revelation.
- **Pitfall:** five-room checklist linearity, repeated combat rooms, or a timer added only because the old pattern expected one.
- **Keep it runnable:** use the optional five-room functional lens above as a mode-and-payoff check, not as a room count; every keyed area points forward or changes a return state.

### 11. Ritual Already In Progress

- **Best when:** the opening should be urgent and magical — a visible magical process already underway.
- **Lore delivery:** the ritual scene itself, chant, sacrifices, unstable portal, possessed object, public omen.
- **Opening:** arrive mid-crisis.
- **Middle:** interrupt, investigate, choose a target, identify what helps or harms.
- **Climax:** ritual pivot — stop, help, or redirect — with a final choice as the magic peaks.
- **Maps:** ritual site, support chamber, final circle or portal.
- **Handouts:** ritual fragment, names list, symbol sketch.
- **Agency:** stop, redirect, bargain, rescue, sabotage.
- **Payoff:** the ritual fails, changes, or becomes a future hook.
- **Pitfall:** ritual rules get arcane.
- **Keep it runnable:** the ritual has 3 visible components players can affect.

### 12. Heist / Recovery

- **Best when:** the fun is approach planning, obstacle layers, and extraction of a specific target.
- **Lore delivery:** stolen item, patron brief, security sketch, captured thief, public display.
- **Opening:** target established and time window shown.
- **Middle:** approach options, obstacle layers, complication, alarm.
- **Climax:** extraction, chase, double-cross, or a reveal inside the target.
- **Maps:** target site, approach route, escape route.
- **Handouts:** floor plan, item sketch, guard schedule, invitation.
- **Agency:** stealth, social entry, force, deception, inside ally.
- **Payoff:** item recovered, an identity revealed, the patron relationship changes.
- **Pitfall:** planning consumes the session.
- **Keep it runnable:** give 3 clear approaches and start the clock after a short planning window.

## Dramatic Situations — The Pressure Library

The pattern library above answers what the table does; a dramatic situation answers why anyone bleeds. Polti's thirty-six situations (1895, public domain) are conflict engines defined by relational roles — and in a bounded adventure they apply to the NPC web the party walks into, never to the party itself. Player characters cannot be assigned emotional arcs; NPCs hold the roles, and the party arrives with a knife that decides how the situation resolves. The roles are the incompatible-wants generator the gate demands.

The thirty-six, compressed for table use:

1. **Supplication** — persecutor, suppliant, an authority whose decision is doubtful. 2. **Deliverance** — the doomed, the threatener, an unexpected rescuer. 3. **Crime pursued by vengeance** — criminal and avenger. 4. **Vengeance for kin upon kin** — justice that splits a family. 5. **Pursuit** — a fugitive and what hunts them. 6. **Disaster** — a power overthrown; the news arrives. 7. **Prey to cruelty or misfortune** — the powerless under a master or fate. 8. **Revolt** — tyrant and conspirators. 9. **Daring enterprise** — a bold objective, a leader, an adversary. 10. **Abduction** — abductor, taken, guardian. 11. **The enigma** — a problem that punishes wrong answers. 12. **Obtaining** — asker and refuser; sometimes an arbitrator. 13. **Enmity of kin** — hatred inside a family. 14. **Rivalry of kin** — two of one blood, one prize. 15. **Murderous adultery** — lovers against a spouse. 16. **Madness** — the mad one and what they break. 17. **Fatal imprudence** — one reckless act, its price. 18. **Involuntary crimes of love** — a bond discovered too late. 19. **Slaying of kin unrecognized** — the killer doesn't know who they kill. 20. **Self-sacrifice for an ideal** — the cause costs everything. 21. **Self-sacrifice for kin** — family bought with the giver's life or soul. 22. **All sacrificed for passion** — a want that consumes duty, wealth, honor. 23. **Necessity of sacrificing loved ones** — the office demands the beloved. 24. **Rivalry of superior and inferior** — unequal contestants, one prize. 25. **Adultery** — the triangle, plain. 26. **Crimes of love** — desire against law or nature. 27. **Discovery of a loved one's dishonor** — the good name was false. 28. **Obstacles to love** — the match everything forbids. 29. **An enemy loved** — the beloved belongs to the other side. 30. **Ambition** — the coveted thing, the cost of reaching. 31. **Conflict with a god** — mortal will against the immortal. 32. **Mistaken jealousy** — the wrong suspicion does real damage. 33. **Erroneous judgment** — the mistake convicts the innocent. 34. **Remorse** — the culprit, the sin, the one who asks. 35. **Recovery of a lost one** — the seeker, the found, what changed. 36. **Loss of loved ones** — the witness to their kin's destruction.

Usage rules:

- Every structure proposal names its **dramatic engine**: one or two situations, and who holds each role ("Supplication: the reeve is the suppliant, the hag the persecutor, the party the authority whose decision is doubtful").
- The fingerprint's pressure-type dimension **is** the named situation — formula-matching becomes checkable, not vibes. (The two adventures shipped before this section existed both ran #21, Self-sacrifice for kin, unlabeled. That is the case for the labels.)
- A proposal slate spans different engines, not one engine in three costumes.
- All thirty-six are in bounds under the Content Register (ben-grade-gate.md). Situations that can drift toward sexual violence in careless hands — abduction, the adultery cluster, crimes of love — inherit hard line two automatically: if a premise touches it, the proposal flags it at Gate 1 for Ben's explicit ruling.
- The engine is design vocabulary. Situation names and numbers never appear in the manuscript — the adventure embodies the situation; it does not cite it.

## Structure Proposal

For any new bounded-adventure concept where the user has not provided a final structure, propose 3 candidate structures before outlining.

Each candidate must state:

- the adventure in one sentence;
- the dramatic engine — the situation(s) powering it, and who holds each role;
- the lore-delivery vehicle;
- the first 10 minutes;
- the part structure;
- maps and handouts;
- likely player approaches;
- climax, payoff, and future hook;
- why it works and why it might fail;
- its fingerprint (defined under Variety below).

### The Structure-Rating Rubric (canonical)

Rate every candidate against all 12 criteria. This is the only structure-rating rubric in the skill.

1. **One-sentence clarity** — the adventure can be stated in one sentence a stranger would understand.
2. **First 10 minutes** — how fast play reaches a real decision, with no backstory required first.
3. **Ease of prep** — how little the DM must hold in memory to run it.
4. **Ease of Roll20 setup** — how cleanly the maps, tokens, and handouts translate to the VTT.
5. **Map usefulness** — the maps this structure needs earn their prep cost in play.
6. **Handout usefulness** — the handouts carry real information players act on.
7. **Player agency** — the structure creates genuine choices, not a corridor.
8. **Challenge clarity** — players can tell what is being asked of them and roughly how hard it looks.
9. **Payoff clarity** — the ending visibly resolves what the opening promised.
10. **Forgotten Realms grounding** — how naturally the structure roots in the Realms (see realms-grounding.md).
11. **Risk of abstraction** — how likely the concept drifts into conceptual sprawl instead of concrete playable scenes; higher risk scores lower.
12. **Catalog variety** — how far the candidate breaks from the recent formula (see Variety below).

### Variety / Anti-Formula

Every candidate records a **fingerprint** of five dimensions: structure pattern, pressure type (the named dramatic situation — see Dramatic Situations above), clock type, climax shape, strange-thing category.

- Compare each candidate's fingerprint against the two most recently shipped adventures (under `one-shots/`). A candidate matching either of them on 3 or more dimensions is penalized on the Catalog variety criterion.
- Every proposal slate must include at least one structure pattern not used in the last two shipped adventures.

### Recommendation

Recommend one structure and explain why it beats the alternatives: what to preserve, what to avoid, the exact lore-delivery vehicle, primary maps, first scene, climax, and payoff.

When rejecting alternatives, say what each would do well and why it is not best for this concept. A rejected structure may still contribute a scene or branch.

Wait for the user's approval of the recommended structure before generating the full packet.
