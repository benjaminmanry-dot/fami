# Ben-Grade Gate

The home of Ben's taste doctrine, the quality gate, and the rating rules. Apply it before treating a one-shot manuscript as table-ready, strong, publishable, or ready for final human review. Scene-craft mechanics live in references/scene-and-check-standard.md, structure ratings in references/structure-patterns.md, encounter and spell math in references/mechanical-floor.md; the gate cites those standards rather than restating them.

## Rating Scale

Ratings: `publishable`, `strong`, `usable`, `weak`, `not acceptable`.

- A one-shot is not final unless the overall rating is `strong` or `publishable`.
- Taste rule: a coherent, runnable, but generic one-shot cannot rate above `usable`. To rate `strong` or `publishable`, it must also pass this gate: specific, strange enough to remember, emotionally pressured, table-actionable, and free of visible design scaffolding in the DM-facing manuscript.
- Evidence rule: `strong` or `publishable` ratings require quoted manuscript evidence per criterion, not general confidence. For every criterion the review claims is met, cite the section, scene, or line that proves it. A quality review that praises tone or coherence without pointing to concrete table proof is itself defective.

## Core Rule

Playable is necessary, but not sufficient.

A Ben-grade one-shot must be specific, strange enough to be remembered, emotionally pressured, and clear enough for a DM to run at the table. If the adventure is merely coherent, balanced, lore-aware, and easy to summarize, it is not finished. The module should make the DM think: "I can run this tonight, and I know why this situation matters."

Two recorded rulings govern everything below:

- "If the output sounds impressive but the DM cannot run it easily, it fails."
- "Module manuscripts should read like adventures, not spreadsheets, forms, or design documents."

## Human Pressure Is the Anchor

"Human pressure is the anchor, not the ceiling."

"Ben-grade Story Engine output should use human pressure as the anchor at every tier, while scaling abstraction, myth, cosmology, and grandeur to player capability. The target is not realism over myth; the target is concrete playable embodiment over conceptual sprawl."

Flattening an adventure into ordinary realism when the tier supports mythic, planar, divine, or cosmic play is a failure, not a safety.

Player capability creates narrative radius:

- **Tier 1, local play:** characters mostly have local tools. Adventures usually concern villages, roads, ruins, families, local monsters, immediate obligations, and visible human cost.
- **Tier 2, regional play:** characters can cross a city, stronghold, or battlefield. Adventures can concern cities, factions, noble houses, duchies, religious institutions, regional curses, and public legitimacy crises.
- **Tier 3, world-scale play:** characters can cross nations or the world. Adventures can concern nations, legendary sites, ancient powers, world-threatening factions, mythic history, and world-scale consequences embodied through a person, oath, place, or visible cost.
- **Tier 4, cosmic play:** characters can act at the scale of gods, afterlives, cosmic engines, planes, universal laws, wish-scale choices, and divine intervention. Adventures can carry divine stakes, planar metaphysics, abstract mythic structures, and grandeur when they remain playable.

Human pressure scales with the tier:

- **Tier 1:** a frightened peasant, missing child, feud, debt, corpse, shameful secret, family obligation, or local betrayal.
- **Tier 2:** a city's loyalty, noble house compromise, temple lie, faction hostage, regional betrayal, or legitimacy crisis.
- **Tier 3:** an empire choosing survival over mercy, a legendary hero's broken oath, a nation remembering the wrong history, or a world-scale consequence embodied in one person.
- **Tier 4:** a god's loneliness, a dead civilization's last witness, a soul-market, a cosmic bargain, or a universal law made visible through one suffering being.

## Single Branch Rule

When many symbolic concepts compete, collapse them into the single concrete branch that carries the adventure's emotional and playable force: "one concrete playable embodiment should carry the abstraction." Prefer one person, object, wound, debt, corpse, broken promise, strange image, or visible cost over piled-up symbolic labels, named concepts, metaphors, and explanatory layers. At higher tiers the branch can be mythic or cosmic — a god's tear, a broken law of death, a single name erased from every afterlife, a mortal witness carrying a cosmic truth. High-tier mythic play is scaled, not suppressed. The point is not to ban abstraction; the point is to make one playable embodiment carry it.

## Taste Doctrine

- Prefer playable situations over lore exposition. A long lore block without a decision is a defect. Setting lore counts only when it creates a job, clue, route, NPC move, pressure, or consequence.
- Every major scene needs a thing players can touch, choose, interrupt, steal, offend, break, rescue, exploit, or misunderstand.
- NPCs should want incompatible things, and NPC wants must become table pressure — offers, demands, threats, lies, prices, tells, movement, or escalation — never only feelings or vibes. Those moves must appear where play happens: a good NPC card is not enough if the scene does not say what that NPC does next.
- Emotional pressure matters. Someone should stand to lose face, safety, love, status, money, memory, land, duty, or future if the characters fail or delay.
- Outlandish is good when it remains usable. Strange details must invite action, clue discovery, risk, route choice, consequence, or a concrete table question. If the memorable weirdness is procedural — a rule, bargain, object, ritual, custom, or hazard — the manuscript needs a compact usable rule near its first use, explicit enough for the table to test it (scene-adjacent procedure guidance in `references/scene-and-check-standard.md`, “Player-facing action” and “Check Adjudication,” and `references/module-format-standard.md`, “First-Use Adjacency”). A strange premise that never becomes a rule, object, route, cost, clue, bargain, or scene move fails.
- "Box text should place characters in action and imply playable options through immediate NPCs, dangers, pressures, objects, exits, and affordances." Keep boxed text minimal; give the DM handles, not speeches.
- "Naked DCs are not acceptable. Every suggested check needs adjudication: success, failure, cost, partial result, and how the scene continues." In particular: "Stealth failure must say who notices, what they do, and whether combat actually starts." (Adjudication format in references/scene-and-check-standard.md.)
- Plain, table-clear language beats forced flavor coinages (recorded example: "false patrol call" was rejected for "false road signal"). Invented terms are allowed only when they change play; grand fantasy nouns must be completed into action, history, emotion, and location, or cut. (Specificity rules in references/realms-grounding.md.)
- Match the prose economy of a professional published module. Recorded ruling (2026-07-02): "the adventure is still a bit more verbose than your average official hardcover or module." Every sentence buys table function or feeling; connective tissue, restated context, and second examples get cut. When two versions both work, the shorter one ships.
- Use concrete names, places, objects, and procedures. No role-label placeholders — "the rival," "the ruins" — in final packet text.
- Prep likely branches, not scripted outcomes. Branches that secretly force the same outcome are a defect.
- The reader must be runnable under time pressure: lead with how to start, preserve one clear mechanical and narrative spine, and place the next useful fact at first use. Do not create a second summary layer or a menu of hypothetical party behavior.
- Recorded ruling (2026-08-25): "We shouldn't write in a ton of allowances for 'what if the party does xyz?'. The DM handles that at the table. The running notes need to give us a thorough mechanical/narrative spine to move the party along. The DM handles the ushering." Ordinary deviations are not missing content when the situation and standard rules let a competent DM adjudicate them.
- Recorded ruling (2026-08-25): "Asides like this need to really justify themselves in order to validate interrupting the flow of the module." Put NPC dialogue with the NPC and special rules where the party first encounters them. Use a detached aside only when one genuinely global procedure must remain visible across separated scenes.
- Challenge philosophy — recorded rulings (2026-07-02): "My combats are hard, but I don't have a lot of player death — and that's because, like a good DM, I am an advocate for my players." / "When I challenge my players, my goal is not for them to 'make believe' heroism, but for them to actually demonstrate the elements of composure under stress, clarity during difficulty, and principled decision-making that is actually necessary to enact heroism in the context of one's own life. It's the party's job to win." / "Flight of the prisoner — not flight of the coward." The manuscript arms the DM-advocate: give scenes the character-available information a DM can legitimately share when players strategize, never punish smart play, and keep running, bargaining, and capture visible as viable alternatives to death (interaction standard in references/mechanical-floor.md). Encounters are hard so that the players' real decisions are what beat them.
- Player handouts should feel like in-world artifacts, not generic parchment; asset prompts must be useful, not merely atmospheric. The setup manifest (templates/roll20_setup_manifest.md) must be concrete enough to set up from directly.

## What Must Be True

Before a one-shot can rate `strong` or `publishable`, the manuscript or quality review must answer:

- What concrete table action starts play in the first 10 minutes?
- What is the one memorable strange thing that could only belong to this adventure?
- Who is hurt, ashamed, desperate, tempted, or under pressure because of the central problem?
- Which of delay, refusal, misreading, failed leverage, force, or stealth failure materially changes this adventure, and what gets worse in those relevant cases?
- What choice costs the characters something other than hit points?
- What does each major NPC want, what pressure tactic do they use, and how do they react when the party disrupts them?
- What physical object, route, room feature, clue, hazard, handout, or rule can players manipulate in each major scene?
- What local names, customs, factions, roads, jobs, debts, laws, or relationships make the setting usable rather than decorative?
- What generic fantasy language has been replaced with plain table language or completed into playable specificity?
- What design scaffolding has been removed from the DM-facing manuscript?

If the answers are weak, revise before polishing layout or assets.

## Table Proof Pass

Before drafting or revising the manuscript, prove the adventure works at the table. Keep this proof in design notes or QA files. Transform only the facts Ben needs into the reader's opening, global procedure, or scene-adjacent prose; never paste rubric language into the manuscript.

The proof must answer:

- What does the DM read or paraphrase first?
- What is the first meaningful player decision inside 10 minutes?
- Which likely forms of pressure matter to this adventure, and what moves when they occur?
- What are the 3-5 scene names, and what changes in each scene?
- What are each important NPC's next two table moves if ignored or challenged?
- What clue has at least two delivery paths?
- What central strange rule, object, hazard, or custom can players test, exploit, bargain with, or break?
- What is the emergency skip path if the table is short on time?

If the proof is fuzzy, do not write a prettier manuscript. Fix the adventure's table behavior first.

## Spine, Not Allowances

During design, consider likely player pressure against the adventure so the central situation does not collapse through an unexamined load-bearing interaction. The manuscript explicitly states only what the DM cannot safely infer from ordinary rules and the concrete facts on the page: custom procedures, counterintuitive effects, special object limits, victory conditions, and consequences that define the moral or mechanical engine.

Do not require a refusal branch, spell-by-spell counter list, generic action menu, or explicit ruling for every plausible improvisation. The review asks whether the manuscript supplies the facts and procedures needed for sound adjudication, not whether it pre-writes the DM's judgment.

Every fact the moral engine rests on — what actually ails the victim, what the villain's death changes, what a custom object can and cannot do — must still be stated in DM-facing text at first use rather than implied or left in design notes.

## Content Register

Recorded ruling (2026-07-02): "Content safety has never been a salient concern at my tables. Player interviews are detailed, and session zeroes are explicit. We run a R-rated game in all respects but the sexual. Crude humor is allowed (even encouraged). Romantic plots, the same — with an obvious fade to black rule for anything more explicit than a chaste kiss."

What this means for generation:

- Write to an R-rated register without flinching: violence, dread, body horror, moral ugliness, crude humor, and romance are all in bounds when the adventure earns them. Do not sand dark material down to PG defaults.
- Hard line one: nothing sexually explicit past fade-to-black. A chaste kiss is the on-screen ceiling.
- Hard line two — recorded ruling (2026-07-02): "Sexual violence of any kind is never used as a cheap plot device. There are better ways to make our villains evil without touching on themes that make survivors (of which over 60% of women are) uncomfortable." Villains are never made evil this way; it is never texture, backstory seasoning, or shock. In the rare case where it is genuinely structural to a premise — the way half-orc existence in Forgotten Realms canon implies it at historical distance — it stays exactly there: implication and consequence in the deep background, never a depicted scene, never dwelt on, and the structure proposal must flag it explicitly at Gate 1 so Ben rules on it before a word of manuscript exists. When in doubt, find the better way.
- Safety lives upstream — in Ben's player interviews and session zeroes — never in packet apparatus. No warning labels, no handling advice, no at-the-table adjustment coaching: Ben has run professional tables for seven years without a moderation incident and does not need the engine's guidance on managing adult themes.
- The cover carries exactly one **Themes:** line — plain information so Ben can match the packet to the right group, nothing more.
- If a packet is ever prepared for public sale rather than Ben's own tables, add market-standard content advisories at that point; not before.

## Scaffold Leakage Ban

The DM-facing manuscript must not expose the engine's working notes. Design scaffolding lives in design notes and QA files only, never in the manuscript:

- pillar tags and design-category labels — "Per-scene pillar tags are unnecessary in the DM-facing module.";
- route matrices, check inventories, and audit tables;
- generic option lists — "'What Characters Can Do' blocks are often a smell if proper scene procedure makes player options obvious.";
- grounding explanations — "DM-facing packets do not need a visible section explaining Forgotten Realms grounding; the adventure should simply be grounded in the Realms." (posture in references/realms-grounding.md);
- asset-generation commentary, and interspersed images — "Generated images should not be interspersed throughout the module body by default." (placement rules in references/module-format-standard.md);
- rubric language of any kind;
- design math — DPR calculations, kill-vector analysis, XP thresholds, and action-economy ratios belong in design notes and the quality review (mechanical-floor.md); the manuscript shows combat as trigger, tactics, and consequences, never as arithmetic;
- instruction-voice register — text that reads as the generator obeying its own standards rather than a module speaking to a DM. Recorded ruling (2026-07-02): "'on a failure, state it exactly' seems more like advice to the agent generating the module than to the DM running the module." The manuscript performs its standards silently: write the failure result itself — who notices, what they do — never an instruction to state it, name it, or keep it honest.

DM orientation is carried by ordinary module sections: Dramatis Personae, Adventure Background, and Adventure Summary. Transform useful scaffolding into scene prose: what the characters perceive, what is happening, what they can try, what changes, and where play points next.

## Rating Caps

Cap the overall rating at `usable` if any of these hold:

- the adventure is coherent but generic;
- the scenes are readable but not emotionally pressured;
- the setting grounding is accurate but decorative;
- NPCs have motives but no table moves;
- the premise has urgency but no human cost;
- checks exist but do not alter scene state;
- the manuscript exposes design scaffolding that makes the module feel assembled rather than written;
- the cover lacks its one-line Themes label (Content Register);
- the strangeness is atmospheric only — memorable weirdness with no playable handle;
- the language is interchangeable — generic fantasy phrasing that could belong to any module;
- the DM can run it, but nothing feels specific, strange, or memorable.

For `strong` or `publishable`, the review must identify the visible consequence loop for delay, refusal, force, stealth failure, and failed social leverage where those paths are relevant; if the manuscript cannot show where those consequences appear, cap the rating at `usable`. (Consequence doctrine in references/scene-and-check-standard.md.)

Quoted manuscript proof is required for each of: the strange fingerprint, human pressure, scene pressure, NPC table moves, the consequence loop, check continuation (fail-forward), scaffold cleanup, tier-scaled abstraction, single-branch embodiment, the first-ten-minute decision, spine sufficiency for load-bearing custom interactions, and the cover Themes line.

## Revision Passes

When a draft is playable but not Ben-grade, do not expand it broadly. Run these passes in order:

1. **Premise pressure pass:** sharpen who is hurt, what is wrong right now, what gets worse, and why the opening matters.
2. **Scene-action pass:** ensure every scene has a player-facing action, a thing to interact with, and a next step.
3. **Consequence pass:** pair checks, stealth, social choices, delay, force, and negotiation with concrete state changes.
4. **NPC pressure pass:** turn NPC motives into offers, threats, prices, tells, lies, movements, and reactions.
5. **Language and scaffold pass:** replace fuzzy phrasing, remove visible design scaffolding, and keep only reference aids that solve a real table problem.

Do not add new major lore, new locations, or new subsystems unless the draft cannot function without them.

## Final Question

Before final approval, ask: would Ben actually want to run this at his table, or would he only respect that it is competent? If the honest answer is "competent," revise the pressure, specificity, consequences, NPC moves, and language before calling the packet strong.
