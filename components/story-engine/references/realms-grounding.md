# Forgotten Realms Grounding

This document sets the setting posture for one-shot generation: when to use Forgotten Realms lore, how much lore weight a one-shot can carry, and how specific the world must be. Scene-level concreteness, box text, and check adjudication are defined in scene-and-check-standard.md. Manuscript section order and reader flow are defined in module-format-standard.md.

## Default Posture

The default table is a private home game set in the Forgotten Realms. Unless the user asks for a different setting or setting-neutral output, ground every one-shot in a concrete Realms context. Realms lore is allowed and encouraged whenever it makes the adventure more alive, concrete, lived-in, or playable. Lore should make the adventure easier to run, not harder; if a lore element makes something harder to run, use a plainer element.

**Grounding is invisible in the deliverable.** DM-facing packets do not need a visible section explaining Forgotten Realms grounding; the adventure should simply be grounded in the Realms. Never add a manuscript section whose purpose is to announce or justify the setting. The adventure uses Realms places, factions, customs, roads, ruins, gods, monsters, laws, and local pressures directly in the playable text. Explicit grounding and provenance notes belong in design notes and QA files only, never in the manuscript.

## Grounding Anchors

Every one-shot must use at least one concrete Realms anchor:

- a region (Sword Coast, Dalelands, Anauroch, Cormyr, Calimshan, Chult, Moonsea, the North, Western Heartlands, or another fit for the premise);
- a named road, trade route, ferry, pass, river, oasis, or port, with its caravan customs and frontier pressures;
- a settlement, ruin, mine, tomb, battlefield, or watchpost;
- a god, church, shrine, saint, taboo, or oath;
- a faction with practical stakes;
- a local political problem, fear, or rumor;
- regional monster pressure or a geographic hazard;
- a historical layer (Netherese, Calishite, Chultan, Mulhorandi, Illefarn, Imaskari, or another suitable dead empire) that explains why a ruin works the way it does.

Prefer anchors that touch daily life: living customs that explain why people act as they do, local politics, and named routes beat encyclopedic history.

## Lore Weight

How much lore a one-shot can carry:

- Every piece of lore in the packet must answer at least one of: why here? why now? who cares? what happens if no one acts? what can the characters do first? what changes at the table?
- If lore is obscure, compress it to one table-usable sentence, then immediately connect it to a scene, NPC, clue, route, or consequence.
- Do not deliver lore as a lecture. No abstract thesis, invented metaphysical terms, or hidden cosmology before the situation is concrete, and never require the DM to explain a lore essay before play can start.
- If the adventure's one-sentence pitch requires invented terminology to make sense, simplify.
- Mythic or invented names must be earned: if a coined term does not create action, history, emotional stakes, spatial presence, or a handout/map/procedure payoff, demote it to plain or local language. Plain language beats coinages — naming rules and the recorded ruling live in references/scene-and-check-standard.md.
- A one-shot includes only the lore needed to run the adventure — nothing held in reserve for a campaign that does not exist.

## Lore As Play

Lore must create or clarify something playable: a job, a route, an NPC agenda, a clue, a hazard, a handout, a faction consequence, a monster pressure, a location feature, or a payoff. Lore that is decoration only gets cut.

The conclusion must pay off the chosen Realms grounding: the anchor has to matter by the end, not just flavor the start.

## Lore-Delivery Vehicle

Every one-shot needs one practical lore-delivery vehicle. It does not need to be an NPC. Options include:

- a practical NPC guide or patron;
- a handout, or a map found on an enemy;
- an ambush that reveals the plot or carries evidence;
- a dead body that shows method and direction;
- a public disaster, strange weather, or visible supernatural event;
- a faction order, a captured prisoner, or a battlefield aftermath;
- a cursed object, a local song or rhyme, or a location feature that reveals the premise.

The vehicle must quickly answer: What is happening? Why should the characters care? Where can they go? What can they do first?

If the vehicle is a plot-facing NPC, give that NPC a "What [NPC] Knows" block. Keep key facts out of stylized dialogue-only presentation.

## Famous Names Are World Gravity

Famous Realms characters, gods, organizations, and places act as world gravity, not decoration — and never solve the adventure for the characters. Do not use celebrity cameos unless the adventure's scale, location, stakes, and payoff justify them. A famous name must not overshadow the party or appear only to prove the setting is the Forgotten Realms.

Good use:

- a famous faction's policy creates a local problem;
- a temple tradition shapes an NPC's decision;
- an old empire explains why a ruin works the way it does;
- a powerful NPC is mentioned as distant pressure, not a convenient answer.

Weak use (revise on sight):

- a famous NPC appears without consequence;
- lore is pasted in without creating action;
- every local problem secretly involves a setting celebrity.

## Specificity

The adventure builds the world; it never describes placeholders. Generic role labels posing as names — "The Rival Tomb-Seeker," "The Oasis Elder," "the broker," "the ruins," "the emissary" — are not acceptable in final running text. If canon lacks a name, invent a concrete one and use it consistently throughout the packet.

Every important NPC, place, object, and faction cell must have:

- a specific name;
- a functional title or role;
- one visual handle;
- one behavioral or voice handle;
- one concrete table move.

Example:

- Weak: "The Rival Tomb-Seeker asks the wind to witness the claim."
- Better: "Sair al-Hadd, brass-tacked surveyor for an unnamed patron, kneels beneath the arch and reads a claim from his copied ledger."

Reach for proper nouns, local titles, physical artifacts, and named routes, doors, bridges, wells, tents, ledgers, seals, patrons, and witnesses — the customs and objects that affect what characters choose to do. Avoid vague asset names that do not match named people and places. For scene-level concreteness — objects in position, first actions, adjudicated outcomes — apply scene-and-check-standard.md.

## Sources and Conflicts

- Prefer user-provided canon first, then official books and sourcebook references. The Forgotten Realms Wiki (https://forgottenrealms.fandom.com/wiki/Main_Page) is a useful starting point for research.
- If the user asks for the latest or most accurate state of a Realms topic, verify with current sources rather than relying on memory.
- Do not copy large passages from any source; summarize in original words. Preserve proper nouns and canonical spellings.
- If canon is edition-sensitive, note the era or edition in the design notes if known. If two sources conflict, do not smooth it over: flag the uncertainty in design notes, offer choices where useful, and keep play moving.
- Researched Realms lore never overrides the user's table canon.
- Source and provenance notes stay in design notes and QA files; the manuscript reads as an adventure, with no citations or research apparatus.
