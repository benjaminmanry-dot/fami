# Ben Table Taste

Use this reference before producing or revising DM prep for Ben's table.

## Core Rules

- Prefer playable situations over lore exposition.
- Every major scene needs a thing players can touch, choose, interrupt, steal, offend, break, rescue, exploit, or misunderstand.
- NPCs should want incompatible things.
- NPC wants should become table pressure: offers, demands, threats, lies, prices, tells, movement, or escalation.
- Avoid generic fantasy filler.
- Outlandish is good when it remains usable.
- Strange details should be playable. They should invite action, clue discovery, risk, route choice, consequence, or a concrete table question.
- Emotional pressure matters. Someone should stand to lose face, safety, love, status, money, memory, land, duty, or future if the characters fail or delay.
- Human pressure is the anchor, not the ceiling. It should ground myth, symbolism, cosmology, and divine stakes rather than suppress them.
- Player capability creates narrative radius. Keep Tier 1 mostly local and visible; let Tier 2 carry regional institutions and legitimacy; let Tier 3 carry nations, legends, and world-scale history; let Tier 4 carry gods, afterlives, cosmic engines, universal laws, and reality-scale choices.
- Large abstractions need one concrete table-facing embodiment: one person, object, wound, debt, corpse, broken promise, strange image, visible cost, or high-tier equivalent such as a god's tear, a broken law of death, or a mortal witness carrying cosmic truth.
- Prep likely branches, not scripted outcomes.
- Roll20 prep should be concrete enough to set up.
- Player handouts should feel like in-world artifacts, not generic parchment.
- The table notes should be runnable under time pressure.
- Keep boxed text minimal; give the DM handles, not speeches.
- Use concrete names, places, objects, and procedures. Avoid role-label placeholders in final packet text.

## Packet Priorities

- Lead with how to start at the table.
- Include a short "DM at the table" summary for each major scene.
- Make "if players go sideways" guidance practical and portable.
- Use concise but vivid prep: enough flavor to run, not paragraphs the DM has to mine.
- Give each scene at least one object, pressure, NPC move, or rule the players can act on.
- Box text should place characters in the action, not merely describe atmosphere. It should imply playable options through visible NPCs, pressures, dangers, objects, exits, and affordances.
- If the session's memorable weirdness is a rule, bargain, object, ritual, local custom, or hazard, make the player-facing handle explicit enough that the table can test it.
- Make the minimum viable Roll20 setup clear.
- Make the adventure feel built: named NPCs, named sublocations, named artifacts, and exact first moves.
- Make the adventure feel worth running: one memorable strange thing, one human cost, one moving pressure, and one choice with a consequence.
- Put important NPC pressure where play happens. A good NPC card is not enough if the scene does not say what that NPC does next.

## Anti-Patterns

- Long lore blocks without a decision.
- Flattening every adventure into ordinary realism when the party tier supports mythic, planar, divine, or cosmic play.
- Piling up symbolic labels, oaths, ledgers, tags, names, metaphors, and abstract systems when one concrete table-facing branch can carry the same feeling.
- Accurate setting lore that does not create a job, clue, route, NPC move, pressure, or consequence.
- Scene descriptions that sound good but do not tell the DM what players can do.
- NPC agendas that are only feelings or vibes.
- NPC backstories that never become offers, threats, lies, prices, reactions, or movement.
- NPCs or locations left as labels like "the rival" or "the ruins" when the adventure needs proper nouns.
- Grand fantasy nouns that are not completed into action, history, emotion, and location.
- Polished but generic adventure prose that a DM respects but does not want to run.
- Asset prompts that are atmospheric but not useful.
- Handouts that could belong to any fantasy campaign.
- Branches that secretly force the same outcome.
- Strange premises that never become a rule, object, route, cost, clue, bargain, or scene move.
- Quality reviews that praise tone or coherence without pointing to concrete table proof.
