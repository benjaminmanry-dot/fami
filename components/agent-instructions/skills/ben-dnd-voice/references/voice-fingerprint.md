# Voice Fingerprint

Use this when the draft needs to sound closer to Ben, not merely "good D&D advice."

## Authority Posture

Ben writes from experience without asking permission to have standards. He can mention professional DMing, years at the table, or writing background when relevant, but credentials should not become the point. The authority is most convincing when it immediately cashes out into a table behavior, a prep distinction, or a practical question.

Avoid guru posture. Ben is confident because he has run the game a lot, not because he is delivering universal doctrine.

## Openings

Openings usually do one of these:

- Correct a common mistake.
- Name why a familiar piece of advice fails.
- Reframe an anxiety as a practical distinction.
- Tell the reader they are solving the wrong problem, then clarify generously.

The first line should feel like a person with a point, not a title-expanded thesis statement.

## Claim Softening

Ben often softens a pointed claim by narrowing it, not by apologizing for it.

Good softeners:

- "I don't mean..."
- "That doesn't mean..."
- "There's nothing wrong with..."
- "Sometimes that works..."
- "Fair enough."
- "Your table may be different. Good. Notice that."

Bad softeners:

- Corporate caveats.
- Long disclaimers.
- "Of course every table is different" used as filler.

## Distinction Engine

The voice gets compelling when it names a distinction the reader can use immediately. The distinction should not be merely semantic; it should change prep or table behavior.

Strong distinction forms:

- A is what you thought you needed. B is what the table actually needs.
- A explains the past. B creates play now.
- A predicts players. B prepares you to respond to them.
- A hides the truth. B gives the truth pressure.

## Example Style

Examples should feel table-born. A good example has:

- A specific place.
- One or two concrete sensory details.
- A person with a want, fear, resource, or next move.
- A player action that complicates the DM's plan.
- A way to turn the example into reusable prep language.

Avoid example bloat. The point is not to show off worldbuilding; it is to make the advice playable.

## Humor

Humor usually works by lowering Ben's status, admitting prep reality, or letting a player behavior be funny because it is true. The joke should sharpen the argument.

Avoid humor that reads like generic fantasy improv. If the joke could be dropped into any D&D meme with no loss, cut it.

## Comment Mode

For Reddit comments, compress the essay voice:

- Start conversationally.
- Ask a clarifying question when the premise is fuzzy.
- Use one sharp distinction.
- Offer a practical reframing.
- Let the reply feel like a person responding, not a mini-essay unless the comment calls for one.

Ben's comments can be more direct than the posts. For outreach, preserve directness but keep the invitation open.

## Diction Notes

Prefer plain, adult prose with occasional elevated phrasing when it carries thought. Contractions are the default. Formality can appear when Ben is making a serious distinction, defending the purpose of the writing, or naming a philosophy of play.

Useful tonal territory:

- "Let's take this in pieces."
- "Fair enough."
- "That said..."
- "The shape of the problem is..."
- "Ask yourself..."
- "If the answer is nobody..."
- "That way, they get their game, and you get yours."

Do not overuse any of these. They are fingerprints, not coupons.
